Gear guide: what's actually popular for a coding setup

August 2026 · updated as prices and best-sellers change
This is not a "my setup" post. I don't own most of what's below yet — see the about page for the actual tools I've built and used. This page is a different, clearly-labeled thing: a researched roundup of what's currently best-selling or most-recommended in each category, for anyone assembling a desk to build software on. Keyboard

The Keychron Q-series (Q1/Q3) comes up repeatedly as the pick for people who want a fully programmable, gasket-mounted board that can map macros to CLI commands and IDE shortcuts. For a first mechanical keyboard without that level of tinkering, the Keychron K2 Pro is the more commonly recommended entry point — wireless, hot-swappable switches, and Mac/Windows-friendly.

If wireless multi-device switching matters more than deep firmware customization, the Logitech MX Mechanical is the low-profile alternative that shows up most often.

Mouse

Logitech dominates this category across the board. The MX Anywhere 3S is the model most often recommended for precision work and multi-device use, with a scroll wheel and DPI range built for long sessions rather than gaming. If you just want the cheapest reliable option, the Logitech M185 is the actual best-seller by order volume.

Monitor

27-inch 4K IPS is the size/resolution combination that keeps coming up specifically for code and terminal text — high enough pixel density (around 163 PPI) that small fonts stay sharp without heavy scaling. The Dell S2725QS is a frequently-cited best-seller in that spec at a non-premium price. The LG 27UP850-W is the pick when color accuracy (factory-calibrated, wide gamut) matters as much as text sharpness.

Chair

The Herman Miller Aeron and Steelcase Leap V2 are the two chairs that show up as the long-term standard for people sitting at a desk all day, but both are a real purchase to commit to (remanufactured Leap V2 units run roughly half the price of new). For a lower-cost starting point, the HON Ignition 2.0 is consistently named among the best-selling ergonomic chairs in this category.

Books

Three that come up over and over in indie-hacker and solo-developer reading lists, for different reasons: The Mom Test (Rob Fitzpatrick) on talking to potential users without fooling yourself, Zero to Sold (Arvid Kahl) as a start-to-finish account of bootstrapping a real SaaS business, and Start Small, Stay Small (Rob Walling) on the "stair-step" approach to building something sustainable without needing outside funding.
